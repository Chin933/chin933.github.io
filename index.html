National AI Policy Framing of AI–Labor Substitution Working paper · click to view figures

I scraped 2,348 AI-related policy initiatives from around the world and used a large language model to track how their texts frame the relationship between AI and labor. The map (Figure 1) shows the share of initiatives in each country that explicitly discuss labor-related issues. Figures 2 and 3 show the policy posture these texts take toward AI's labor impacts: developmental framing focuses on improving labor capacity through skills, training, and education, while defensive framing treats AI as a source of job loss, displacement, or worker-protection concerns.

Did the Violence Pay? Estimating the Cost of Coercive Monopoly Enforcement in the 18th-Century VOC Working paper · click to view figure

When does it pay to use violence to make a market? I answer this question using the eighteenth-century VOC monopoly in nutmeg, mace, and cloves. The case is unusually direct: the same trading system records European prices, Asian procurement costs, shipped quantities, voyages, and the military presence used to protect the monopoly.

I combine Posthumus and Zwart Amsterdam prices with BGB Batavia accounting records, DAS voyage links, GZMVOC maritime data, and garrison evidence for Ambon and Banda. I construct a commodity-year panel and estimate gross rent as (PAmsterdam − MCAsia) × Q.

I then ask how much rent the VOC would lose if enforcement stopped and prices fell to marginal cost. This rent loss is the maximum amount the firm should be willing to spend on enforcement, and I compare it to annual enforcement costs from land garrisons and naval manpower.

The comparison shows that coercion was privately profitable: even in the highest-cost year, estimated enforcement costs are about 25 percent of the rent protected by monopoly.
